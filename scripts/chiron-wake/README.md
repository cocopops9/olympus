# Chiron's wake-up

**Current version (video):** `chiron-wake.webp` is now 65 frames of 384 px, 9 by 8, 32 a second
(2 s). It comes from a 2.1 s video generated by Wan 2.2 (Hugging Face Space
`mcp-tools/wan-2-2-first-last-frame`, seed 526625748) with the mirrored sleeping portrait as first
frame and the awake portrait as last, so the design stays the same throughout. `video_sprite.py`
cuts each frame out of its white background (white pockets between the legs included), adds a frame
between each two by optical flow, puts the exact portraits as first and last frame, and packs the
sheet. Before the cut-out each video frame is cleaned (non-local means against the compression
blocks, then an unsharp mask): the video is 480 px and soft next to the portraits, and the cells
are 384 px because the figure is drawn at about 400 to 450 device pixels on a phone.
`restore.py` is an optional stronger pass (Real-ESRGAN x4plus_anime_6B, run before packing with
`RESTORED=1`); it needs torch and the model's weights. The drawings pipeline below is the previous
version, kept for reference.


`frontend/src/assets/chiron-wake.webp` is the wake-up as a sprite sheet: 48 frames of 256 px in an
8 by 6 grid, played at 36 frames a second (1.33 s) by `components/ChironFigure.jsx`, on a canvas, at
every screen refresh, with the two frames around the moment blended. It is a quick wake-up on
purpose: ten key poses (asleep, head up, hands down, up on the forelegs, standing, awake), with the
long stretching poses of the first 6 s version left out.

How it was made (the scripts are the ones used, with paths as they were on the machine that ran them):

1. Twenty-two drawings of the movement, from lying asleep to standing awake, were made in Canva
   (an image generated from the two neighbouring poses each time, so the style stays the same).
   Each was upscaled 4x (Real-ESRGAN, `digital-art-4x`, through ncnn) and cut out from its white
   background with `matte.py`, then scaled to 640 px and set on a common ground line.
2. `short.py` (the 1.33 s sheet; `interpolate.py` is the older 6 s, 144 frame version) follows the motion of every part between neighbouring drawings (dense optical
   flow, OpenCV DIS) and renders the frames along a timeline: each step between drawings
   has its own duration and its own cross-fade window, set from how well the flow could follow the
   motion (the error of the first drawing moved onto the second). Where an arm travels far the flow
   cannot follow it, so that step is quick and its window narrow: the double image lasts one frame
   and reads as motion blur instead of a flicker. A monotone cubic through the drawing times makes
   the speed change smoothly instead of stopping at each drawing. A frame is the neighbouring
   drawing moved along the flow, the two cross-fading only in the middle of a step. `order.json`
   lists the drawings (name, mirrored or not), `seq.py` has the loading (it also removes white
   pockets the cut-out missed, between an arm and the body) and the warping. Earlier versions
   (64 frames in 2.7 s, 108 at 18 a second, 144 over 6 s) were too frantic, too jerky, then too slow
   and full of useless poses; with only a few key poses each step now covers a visible movement.
3. `sprite.py` packs the 48 frames into the sheet.

The drawings were made facing left, except the last two, which face right like the awake portrait;
they are mirrored in step 2 so the whole movement faces right. The drawings themselves are not
kept in the repository, only the result.
